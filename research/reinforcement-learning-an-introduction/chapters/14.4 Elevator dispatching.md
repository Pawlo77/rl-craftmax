# Elevator dispatching (Sutton & Barto, Chapter 14, case study 14.4)

> [!abstract] TL;DR
> Four elevators in a ten-floor building are controlled by Q-learning, extended to continuous time (a semi-Markov decision process, SMDP) and paired with a neural network, which minimizes the squared waiting time of passengers. Trained only in simulation, the learned dispatchers beat the industry-style baseline and several heuristics on every reported measure.

## Problem

- **Decision**: which elevator should stop where, and where idle elevators should wait, so that passengers wait little.
- **Too big for classical methods**: with roughly quantized positions, directions, speeds, buttons and request ages, the system has over $10^{22}$ states.
  - A dynamic-programming sweep ([[04 Dynamic programming]]) at one state per microsecond would take over 1000 years.
- **How dispatchers are built in practice**: designed by hand (heuristics) and evaluated on detailed simulators.
  - A simulator models each car's physics in continuous time and passenger arrivals as discrete random events, with arrival rates changing over a simulated day.
  - The hardest periods are the morning and evening rush hours; dispatchers are designed mainly for those.
- **Performance measures**, all per average passenger:
  - **Waiting time**: time until boarding. **System time**: time until being dropped off.
  - **Share of passengers waiting over 60 seconds.**
  - **Average squared waiting time**, the objective used here: it keeps waits low and encourages fairness, because one very long wait costs far more than several short ones.

## Conceptual framework

- **Down-peak traffic**: the evening rush hour, in which most passengers travel down to the lobby; it is the traffic pattern used for training and testing.
- **Semi-Markov decision process (SMDP)**: a continuous-time decision problem treated as a discrete-time one, jumping from one moment at which a decision is needed to the next.
  - It is possible here because each elevator rarely has a choice (see Approach).
  - Apart from variable time gaps, it is treated like an ordinary Markov decision process ([[03a MDP setting and returns]]), with the reward of a transition equal to the integral of reward over the interval.

## Approach

The method is one-step Q-learning ([[06 Temporal-difference learning]]) with several additions.

1. **Independent agents**: each elevator decides for itself, using its own view of the state.
2. **Constrained actions**: an elevator may not skip a floor where one of its passengers wants out, and may not reverse until all passengers heading its way have been served.
   - It may not stop at a floor without someone getting on or off, nor to pick up at a floor where another car is already stopped.
   - Given a choice between moving up and down, it always moves up; otherwise evening rush traffic would push all cars to the lobby.
   - The last three rules (stop only when someone gets on or off, no joint pickup stops, always up) were included explicitly as prior knowledge to simplify learning.
   - Net effect: the only choice is whether to stop at an approaching floor that has waiting passengers; at all other times no decision is needed.
3. **Reward**: the negative of the sum of squared waiting times of all waiting passengers, as a continuous-time rate; the SMDP return discounts it with $\beta$ (see Math).
4. **Neural network for the action values**: a nonlinear network trained by backpropagation represents $Q$ ([[09a Function approximation and linear methods]]).
   - **Architecture**: 47 input units, 20 hidden units, two output units (one per action: stop or continue).
   - **Input encoding**, which was found critical:
     - 18 units for the nine down-pickup hall buttons: per button, one real-valued unit with the elapsed time since it was pressed and one binary unit that is on if it was not pressed.
     - 16 units, one per possible location and direction of the car that must decide; exactly one is on.
     - 10 units showing the other elevators superimposed over the ten floors; each car leaves a "footprint" by direction and speed (a stopped car activates only its floor, a moving car also the floors it approaches, most strongly the closest). Which car is where is not given.
     - 1 unit: on if the deciding car is at the highest floor with a waiting passenger.
     - 1 unit: on if the deciding car is at the floor of the longest-waiting passenger.
     - 1 bias unit, always on.
5. **Two architectures**:
   - **RL1**: each elevator has its own network and action-value function, so cars could in principle specialize (they tended to learn the same strategy).
   - **RL2**: one shared network learns from the experience of all four cars, so it learns faster.
   - In both, cars act independently but share one reward signal; this adds noise, since a car's reward partly depends on other cars' actions it cannot control.
6. **Training**: simulated evening rush hours, choosing actions with the Gibbs softmax rule ([[02 Multi-armed bandits]]) with the temperature lowered gradually; temperature zero in test runs.
   - Cost: about four days of computing on a 100 mips (million instructions per second) processor, equal to about 60,000 hours of simulated time, which is negligible compared with dynamic programming over the full state set.
7. **Reward when counts are unknown**: a real elevator knows how long each button has been pressed but not how many people wait there.
   - A simulator knows the counts, and the best results used them.
   - Using the button's age together with an estimated arrival rate to compute the expected summed squared waiting time per floor worked nearly as well.

## Results

Comparison on simulated down-peak traffic; the dispatchers include an industry-style one (SECTOR), several heuristics, research algorithms that repeatedly run complex optimization algorithms on-line, and RL1 and RL2. The book prints bars, not numbers; the values below are read from the bars and approximate.

| Measure | SECTOR | Heuristic and optimization dispatchers | RL1 and RL2 |
|---------|--------|----------------------------------------|-------------|
| Average waiting time (system time) | about 46 (69) | about 45-50 (about 63-71) | about 42 (58-59) |
| Percent waiting over 1 minute | about 1.15 | from about 0.1 to 2.7 (worst: DLB) | about 0.1 |
| Average squared waiting time | about 690 | about 350-670 | about 320 |

- Reinforcement learning compares favorably on all measures.
- The two architectures are close on every measure.

## Math

**Notation.** Time is continuous in this case study, so the usual symbols appear in continuous form.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $S$, $A$, $S'$ | state and action at a decision time $t_1$, and the state at the next decision time $t_2$ |
| $R_\tau$ | instantaneous reward at continuous time $\tau$: the negative sum of squared waiting times of all waiting passengers |
| $G_t$ | return from time $t$ (a discounted integral here) |
| $\beta > 0$ | continuous-time discount rate; plays the role of $\gamma$ ([[03a MDP setting and returns]]) |
| $Q$ | tabular or approximate action-value function |
| $\alpha$ | step size |

**Discounted integral return** (replaces the discounted sum):

$$
G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k+1} \quad \text{becomes} \quad G_t = \int_0^\infty e^{-\beta \tau} R_{t+\tau}\, d\tau
$$

- Left: the usual discrete-time return; right: the continuous-time version, with instantaneous reward $R_{t+\tau}$.

**Semi-Markov Q-learning backup** (tabular form):

$$
Q(S,A) \leftarrow Q(S,A) + \alpha \left[ \int_{t_1}^{t_2} e^{-\beta(\tau - t_1)} R_\tau\, d\tau + e^{-\beta(t_2 - t_1)} \min_a Q(S',a) - Q(S,A) \right]
$$

- It estimates the optimal action values of the SMDP; the bracket is the TD error, as in Q-learning ([[06 Temporal-difference learning]]).
- The next-state term uses $\min_a$ as the book prints it; the greedy action is the one this extreme selects.
- $e^{-\beta(t_2-t_1)}$ is a variable discount factor that depends on the time elapsed between decisions.
- With a network, the same bracket drives a gradient step on the network weights instead of a table update ([[09b Control with approximation, bootstrapping]]).

## Dataset example

One row per decision step of one elevator (a decision is needed only when it approaches a floor with a waiting passenger); illustrative values, not printed by the book. The book gives the encoding structure below but not example inputs.

| Input group | Units | Illustrative value at one decision |
|-------------|-------|------------------------------------|
| down-pickup hall buttons | 18 | floor 7: elapsed 10 s, not-pressed unit off; floor 3: elapsed 30 s, not-pressed unit off; other 7 buttons: elapsed 0, not-pressed unit on |
| own car location and direction | 16 | exactly one unit on (car approaching floor 7, moving up) |
| other cars' footprints | 10 | small positive activations on floors the other cars approach, no car identity |
| deciding car at the highest floor with a waiting passenger | 1 | 0 (the car is approaching floor 7, not yet at it) |
| deciding car at floor of longest-waiting passenger | 1 | 0 |
| bias | 1 | 1 |

- **Mapping**: the 47 values form the state $S$; the two network outputs are $Q(S, \text{stop})$ and $Q(S, \text{continue})$, and the softmax rule picks the action $A$ (or the greedy action at temperature zero).
- **Reward for the interval to the next decision** (formalized here): assume one passenger at each of the two pressed buttons and 5 s until the next decision, with ages growing at one second per second. Ignoring discounting ($\beta \approx 0$), the cost is $\int_0^5 (10+\tau)^2 d\tau + \int_0^5 (30+\tau)^2 d\tau = 791.7 + 5291.7 = 6083.3$, so the reward is about $-6083$ in units of squared seconds times seconds (the book gives no units; the reward integrates squared waiting times over time).
- **Absent**: the other cars' decisions, which enter only through the shared reward and the footprint inputs.

## Where it matters in practice

### Signs a problem fits

- The case shows the SMDP device (discounted integral return, variable discount) and a neural-network $Q$ working together; [[14.5 Dynamic channel allocation]] reuses the SMDP formulation and [[14.1 TD-Gammon]] is a neural-network value-function case.
- Several agents act mostly independently, share one cost, and decisions are infrequent and event-driven rather than on a clock.
- A detailed simulator exists, so millions of simulated decisions are affordable.

### What you need to use it

- A simulator and a cost signal accumulated over time, ideally computable from what the real system observes (the arrival-rate estimate shows how).
- A state encoding designed with care: it was critical, and many variants were tried.
- Prior knowledge as constraints on the action set.

### When not to trust it

- The optimal policy is unknown and commercial dispatchers are proprietary, so the learned dispatchers "appeared to perform very well" is the strongest claim available.

## Assumptions & limitations

- **Simulation only**: the learned dispatchers were assessed on simulated traffic, and training used only down-peak rush hours.
- **Heavy prior knowledge**: action constraints, independent agents and a hand-built state encoding made the problem tractable; learning started from a strongly simplified problem.
- **Non-stationary from each car's view**: other cars learn and act at the same time, so each car's reward is noisier.
